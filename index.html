A structural reading of Bloomington, Indiana's rental market

Upzoning didn't build starter homes.
It built mini-dorms.

Indiana University enrolls roughly 45,000 students against a permanent population of about 35,000. When the city loosened zoning to allow duplexes and triplexes by right, the market didn't respond with modest infill for local families — it responded with per-bed suite housing priced for parental capital, not local wages.

Section 01 — Bifurcation

One rental market, two economies that never touch.

Bloomington isn't a single housing market with a supply problem. It's two submarkets sharing a zip code — one priced against local paychecks, one priced against parental wealth and loan disbursements. New density gets built for whichever one pays more per square foot, and that's never the resident market.

Submarket A

The Student Renter

≈ 33,000 people / off-campus

  • Priced in beds, not units. A four-bedroom suite leasing at $1,500 a bed clears $6,000 a month from one footprint a family unit would rent for $2,000.
  • Capital-bundled demand. Rent is backed by parental income and loan disbursements, decoupled from local wages entirely.
  • 12–36 month horizon. Little stake in school quality, street maintenance, or long-run neighborhood stability — the lease outlives the interest.

Submarket B

The Permanent Resident

≈ 35,000 people / local wages

  • Bound to local pay. A mortgage or rent has to be amortized against Bloomington-metro wages — nurses, teachers, city staff, service workers.
  • Bidding against commercial underwriting. A family's pre-approval can't compete with a developer's pro forma built on eight-bed cash flow.
  • No filtering relief. Older housing stock doesn't "trickle down" — it gets re-underwritten as a future student-bed site instead.

Section 04 — The Statutory Vacuum

Indiana leaves the city no lever to pull.

In most states, a city facing this kind of price divergence can attach conditions to new density — reserve some units below market, or cap rent growth. Indiana's legislature has taken both tools off the table statewide.

IC 36-1-24.2

Indiana Code preempts municipalities from enacting inclusionary zoning — the state prohibits local governments from requiring developers to set aside units at below-market rents, and separately preempts local rent stabilization ordinances entirely.

✕ Unavailable

Affordability set-asides

Cannot require any share of new duplex or triplex units to be reserved at below-market rent.

✕ Unavailable

Rent stabilization

Cannot cap year-over-year rent growth even in neighborhoods experiencing acute displacement pressure.

✓ Available

Zoning & typology control

Can still regulate bedroom counts, bathroom ratios, parking, and lot coverage — the levers this report leans on in its policy section.

Section 05 — Underneath The Street

"Growth pays for growth" assumes the pipes agree.

Core neighborhoods built between 1920 and 1960 carry 6–8 inch clay sewer lines sized for single-family peak flow. A one-time connection fee covers the tap — not the trunk-line and lift-station capacity eight simultaneous morning showers actually require.

Section 07 — What The City Can Still Do

Four levers that don't require state permission.

With inclusionary zoning and rent stabilization off the table, the remaining tools are physical, not financial — regulate what gets built, not what it costs.

  1. 01

    Cap bedrooms per unit

    Limit duplex and triplex units in residential core overlays to two bedrooms, and prohibit en-suite bathrooms on every bedroom.

    Objective: remove the economic basis for the eight-bed pro forma entirely.

  2. 02

    Price the pipe, not just the tap

    Assess connection fees against a property's peak hydraulic load — shower and toilet count — not a flat per-unit rate.

    Objective: internalize downstream sewer and lift-station upgrade costs onto the developer.

  3. 03

    Cap street parking permits

    Limit residential permits to two per parcel regardless of unit count, and cap impervious rear-yard parking coverage at 20%.

    Objective: make high-vehicle-count student conversions self-limiting.

  4. 04

    Land-bank around the statute

    Fund a municipal land bank or Community Land Trust to buy modest homes directly and deed-restrict them for owner-occupancy.

    Objective: enforce affordability through land ownership where state law forbids enforcing it through zoning.
